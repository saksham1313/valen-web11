Why Your Back Still Looks Small
(Even Though You Never Skip Back Day)

By John Arthur · Last updated September 9, 2026

You show up for back day every single week. You pull, you row, you feel a pump. And your back still doesn't look like it's putting in the same work as your chest or arms. No width, no thickness, nothing that reads as "trained" from behind.

There's a specific, well-documented reason for this, and it usually isn't a lack of effort. It's that the muscle doing most of the work in your pulling exercises often isn't the one you think it is, and the back is actually several functionally different regions that need different movements to grow.

Your back is width, thickness, and a shelf, and they're three different muscles

The lats are what give your back width, the V-taper look. They run from your lower back and spine up to your upper arm, and they're the primary target of any vertical pulling movement, pull-ups, pulldowns, and similar.

Thickness, the dense look when someone's back is photographed from the side, comes mostly from the mid trapezius and rhomboids, which sit between your shoulder blades and pull them together. These respond best to horizontal pulling, rows in particular.

The upper traps, which build the "shelf" at the base of your neck, get worked by shrugging motions and heavy pulling in general, but they're also loaded during pressing and carrying, which means they can accumulate fatigue from your entire week, not just back day.

The real reason your lats aren't doing the work

Here's the part almost nobody gets told directly: the most common failure in back training isn't picking the wrong exercises, it's that the biceps, forearms, and upper traps quietly take over the movement before the lats ever get properly loaded.

This happens because the lats are large, sit underneath other muscles, and are genuinely hard to feel working, especially compared to biceps, which fire obviously on almost any pulling motion. If you pull with your hands and think about bending your elbow, your arms and upper traps will happily do the job for you, and your lats will get a fraction of the intended stimulus.

The fix that actually works is deliberately pulling with your elbows rather than your hands, thinking of your hands as hooks, and pulling your shoulder blades down and back before you initiate the movement. It sounds like a small cue. It changes which muscle actually does the work.

Vertical pulling and horizontal pulling aren't interchangeable

Pull-ups and lat pulldowns
Vertical pulling, lats as the primary mover, biceps and upper back as assistance. This is the width-building movement pattern.

Barbell rows, cable rows, chest-supported rows
Horizontal pulling, mid trapezius and rhomboids as primary movers, lats assisting depending on elbow path. This is the thickness-building pattern.

Shrugs and heavy deadlifts
Upper trapezius as the primary target, on top of whatever it's already getting from your other pulling and pressing work.

A program built almost entirely around one of these two directions, usually vertical pulling because pull-ups feel like the "real" back exercise, develops one dimension of the back while leaving the other underdeveloped. That's a big part of why some lifters have decent width but no thickness, or the reverse.

Why your traps might be more fatigued than you realize

Because upper traps get loaded during pressing, carrying, and any heavy pull, not just dedicated shrugs, they can accumulate more total weekly fatigue than lats, which typically only get worked during actual back sessions.

This creates a subtle trap: you can walk into a back session assuming your whole back is fresh, when your traps are actually still carrying fatigue from Monday's heavy squats or presses, while your lats are fully recovered and could handle more direct volume than you're giving them.

A single "back: 70% recovered" number can't tell you which of these is true. It's an average of regions with genuinely different fatigue histories that week.

What actually fixes an underdeveloped back

Two things, done together. First, fix the mind-muscle connection on your pulling exercises so the lats are actually doing lat work instead of handing it off to biceps and traps. Second, make sure your programming includes both vertical and horizontal pulling in real working sets, not one as the main lift and the other as an afterthought.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I feel my biceps or traps more than my lats when I row or pull?

This is one of the most common back-training issues. Without deliberately pulling with your elbows and depressing your shoulder blades first, the biceps, forearms, and upper traps take over the movement because they're easier to activate than the lats.

Do lats and traps recover at the same speed?

No. Traps get worked indirectly during shrugs, pressing, and carries in addition to back day, so they can accumulate fatigue faster across a week than lats, which are typically only loaded during dedicated pulling movements.

Do I need both vertical pulling and horizontal pulling for a balanced back?

Yes. Vertical pulls bias the lats for width, horizontal pulls bias the mid-back and rhomboids for thickness. Programs built around only one direction develop one dimension of the back while leaving the other behind.

Can a whole-back recovery score hide an imbalance between lats and traps?

Yes. A single back score averages lats, traps, rhomboids, and rear delts together. Subzone-level tracking separates them so an overworked trap and an undertrained lat show up as two different numbers.
