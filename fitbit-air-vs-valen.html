Fitbit Air vs Valen:
Whole-Body Tracking vs Muscle-Level Recovery

Google's Fitbit Air is a twelve-gram screenless tracker built to disappear on your wrist and quietly log your biology 24/7. Valen is a free app built to tell you exactly which muscles are ready to train. They're not really competing products, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Here's what each one actually does.

What Fitbit Air Does

Fitbit Air launched in May 2026 at $99.99, with a Stephen Curry special edition at $129.99. It's the smallest Fitbit ever made, worn passively around the clock rather than glanced at like a smartwatch. It uses high-fidelity sensors to track 24/7 heart rate, heart rhythm with Afib alerts, SpO2, resting heart rate, heart rate variability, and sleep stages, syncing to the Google Health app on Android or iOS.

A Google Health Premium subscription, $9.99 a month or $99.99 a year with a three-month trial included, unlocks the full AI Coach experience, though the core tracking works on the device alone without it.

What Valen Does

Valen has no hardware and no sensors. It runs on what you log in the gym. Every set gets mapped to specific muscle subzones and processed through a Stimulus-Recovery-Adaptation model, giving you a front and back body heatmap that shows exactly which muscles are recovered, which are still fatigued, and roughly when each one will be ready again.

It has no idea what your resting heart rate is or how well you slept. It only knows what you actually trained, which is a different, narrower, and for lifters specifically, often more actionable slice of information.

Where They Actually Overlap, and Where They Don't

Fitbit Air is a passive, whole-body wellness device. It's built to be worn always, and it's genuinely good at the thing it's built for: baseline heart health, sleep patterns, and long-term trend tracking that has nothing to do with which exercises you did.

Valen is an active, training-specific tool. It only knows anything the moment you log a workout. If you don't train, it has nothing to say about your recovery, because unlike a wearable it isn't reading your body directly, it's reading the stimulus you gave it.

So the honest framing is: Fitbit Air answers "how is my body doing in general," and Valen answers "what did my last few sessions specifically cost each muscle, and when will it be paid back." Neither one covers the other's job. Valen doesn't sync with Fitbit or Google Health today, so if you want both signals you're checking two separate apps.

Fitbit Air answers How's my sleep, heart rate, and general wellness trending, worn passively 24/7.
Valen answers Which specific muscle subzones are recovered enough to train again, based on what you logged.

Cost, Side by Side

Fitbit Air: $99.99 hardware ($129.99 for the special edition), plus an optional $9.99/month or $99.99/year Google Health Premium subscription for the full coaching experience.

Valen: free to download, with the recovery heatmap, workout logging, and exercise library included at no cost. Pro is $24.99 a year or $49.99 as a one-time lifetime purchase, no hardware required.

So Which One Do You Need?

If you want passive, always-on health tracking without needing to lift a finger, and you don't mind wearing something around the clock, Fitbit Air is built for exactly that, and it's genuinely one of the more comfortable ways to do it.

If your actual question is "can I train shoulders today," a heart-health wearable was never going to answer that, no matter how good its sensors are, because it doesn't know your training history. That's specifically what Valen tracks, for free, with nothing to strap on.

If you want both, they're not mutually exclusive. You'd just be running two apps for two different, honestly non-overlapping jobs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Fitbit Air know which muscles I trained?

No. Fitbit Air only reads biometric signals, heart rate, HRV, SpO2, sleep. It has no information about your workouts or which muscles you used.

Do I need to buy hardware to use Valen?

No. Valen has no sensors and needs no wearable. Recovery is calculated entirely from what you log in the gym, through a Stimulus-Recovery-Adaptation model.

How much does each one cost?

Fitbit Air is $99.99 for the hardware ($129.99 for the special edition), plus an optional $9.99/month or $99.99/year Google Health Premium subscription. Valen is free to download with the recovery heatmap included; Pro is $24.99/year or $49.99 one-time, with no hardware to buy.

Can Fitbit Air and Valen work together?

They can be used side by side, but they don't sync with each other today. Fitbit Air answers how your body is doing overall; Valen answers which specific muscles are ready to train based on your logged sessions.

Valen is free on Android. The muscle-level recovery map works entirely from your training log, no wearable purchase required.
