Whoop vs Valen:
Do You Need a Wearable to Know You're Recovered?

Short answer: Whoop and Valen aren't really fighting for the same job. One reads your body. The other reads your training. Most people asking "should I get a Whoop" actually want the second thing and don't realize it yet.

This isn't a "Valen beats Whoop" post. Whoop does something Valen genuinely doesn't do, and Valen does something Whoop genuinely doesn't do. Here's the honest breakdown, so you know which one actually answers the question you're asking.

What Whoop Actually Measures

Whoop is a wrist-worn wearable that produces a single daily Recovery score, color-coded green, yellow, or red. It's built from heart rate variability captured during your deepest sleep, compared against your personal 14-day rolling baseline, plus resting heart rate, sleep duration and quality, respiratory rate, and skin temperature.

That's a real, useful signal. It's telling you how your nervous system and sleep quality are trending overall. What it isn't telling you is anything about your quads specifically, or your rear delts, or whether the volume you did on legs Tuesday was actually enough to justify the soreness you're still feeling Thursday. Whoop has no idea what exercises you did. It only knows your body's overall stress response.

What Valen Actually Measures

Valen has no sensors and no wearable. It runs entirely on your logged training. Every set you log gets mapped to specific muscle subzones, upper chest, lateral delt, long head triceps, and processed through a Stimulus-Recovery-Adaptation model that tracks fatigue per subzone and decays it in real time based on volume, intensity, and that muscle's individual recovery rate.

That's the flip side of Whoop's blind spot. Valen has no idea if you slept four hours or eight, or whether your HRV tanked from a stressful week at work. It only knows what you trained and how hard. If your legs are green but your shoulders are red, that's a training-history fact, not a biometric guess.

Whoop knows Your sleep quality, HRV trend, resting heart rate, and overall systemic stress load.
Valen knows Exactly which muscle subzones you trained, how hard, and how long each one needs before it's ready again.

The Question That Actually Matters

If the question is "am I run down, stressed, or under-slept in general," Whoop's Recovery score is a legitimate answer, and Valen genuinely can't give you that without a wearable's sensors.

But if the question is "can I train shoulders today or is it still cooked from Tuesday," a whole-body green Recovery score doesn't answer that. It can't. It has no idea you did overhead press two days ago. That's the specific gap Valen was built to close, and it's the question most lifters are actually asking when they say they want to "know if they're recovered."

Cost

Whoop is membership-only. Plans run roughly $199 to $359 a year depending on tier, and the hardware is bundled into that cost rather than sold separately.

Valen is free to download, with the full recovery heatmap and muscle-subzone tracking included in the free tier. Pro, which adds full history, weekly planning, and deeper insights, is $24.99 a year or $49.99 as a one-time lifetime purchase. No hardware to buy at all.

Honest Verdict

If you want a general readiness signal from your sleep and nervous system, and you're fine paying an annual membership for it, Whoop does that job well.

If what you actually want to know is which specific muscles are ready to train today, Whoop can't answer that regardless of what color your Recovery ring shows, because it was never looking at your training in the first place. That's what Valen tracks, for free, with nothing to strap on.

Plenty of people would genuinely benefit from both. They measure different halves of the same problem. Valen doesn't sync with Whoop today, so if you run both you'll be checking two apps, not one combined view, but neither one is lying to you about what it does and doesn't know.

Valen is free on Android. The recovery heatmap and muscle-subzone tracking work without any wearable, sensor, or hardware purchase.
